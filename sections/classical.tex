In this section we will introduce the classical smi-online model introduced by Karp et al~\cite{karp}

In this model the two anticliques of the bipartite graph are divided into offline and online agents.
Offline agents are vertices that belong to one anticlique and are always visible.
Online agents are the vertices from the other anticlique and arrive in the online fashion.
When an online agent arrives edges incident to it are revealed.

The adversary is oblivious as an adaptive adversary is trivial.
\begin{remark}
	No algorithm has a competitiveness asymptotically higher than $\frac{1}{2}$ against an adaptive adversary
\end{remark}
\begin{proof}
Begin with $2n$ offline agents $V$ and $k=0,X_0=\emptyset$.\\
Adversary repeats the following $n$ times: reveals an agent $u$ connected to $V\backslash X_k$. $k=k+1$, for some unmatched $v\in V; V_{k+1}=V_k\cup\{v\}$.\\
Then adversary reveals $n$ agents $U'$ connected to already matched offline agents. The ultimate size of the matching is $n$.

In the final graph (figure), all matched vertices $V'$ are connected to all online agents and all unmatched $v_i$ agents are connected to all $u_j$ such that $j\leq i$. Clearly, it is possible to match $v_i$ to $u_i$ and $V'$ to $U'$, forming a matching of size $2n$\\
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
	In the semi-online model, any deterministic algorithm has a competivineness no greater than $\frac{1}{2}$, there is a deterministic algorithm with competitiveness of $\frac{1}{2}$
\end{remark}
\begin{algorithm}
	\caption{Naive}
\begin{algorithmic}
	\item on Arrival of vertex $v$: match it with any offline agent if available
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\begin{proof}
	Trivially the competitiveness cannot be greater than $\frac{1}{2}$ as against a deterministic algorithm there is no difference between an adaptive and an oblivious adversary.

	To prove that there is a deterministic algorithm with competitiveness of $\frac{1}{2}$ consider the a naive algorithm.
	
	for every $(u,v)\in E(G)$ either $u$ or $v$ is matched. Therefore at least half of all vertices that could be matched are matched, therefore the comptititveness isn't lower than $\frac{1}{2}$
\end{proof}

The main discovery of the Karp paper is the following algorithm

\begin{algorithm}
	\caption{Ranking}
	\begin{algorithmic}
		\item on Initialization: create a random permutation of the offline agents $\sigma$
		\item on Arrival of vertex $v$: match it with the first availible offline neighbor according to $\sigma$
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

as well as the following theorems

\begin{theorem}
	Ranking has asymptotic competitiveness of $1-\frac{1}{e}$
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}
	No algorithm has asymptotic competitiveness higher than $1-\frac{1}{e}$
\end{theorem}

for offline vertex $v$ $\sigma(v)$ is the rank of $v$
